================================================================================
NOBLES & KNIGHTS - DESIGN DIRECTION
Living document. Single source of truth for game direction.
================================================================================

PURPOSE
-------
This document describes what Nobles & Knights should be - its intent, systems,
and reasoning. Code state lives in the code; this document captures design
direction.

STATUS VOCABULARY
-----------------
  [LIVE]    Implemented, and does what this document says.
  [PARTIAL] Implemented, but incomplete against the intent described here.
  [DESIGN]  Designed on paper. Not implemented.
  [ABSENT]  Neither designed nor implemented.
  [OPEN]    Actively undecided. Do not assume an answer.

================================================================================
1. VISION
================================================================================

Nobles & Knights is a slow, character-driven simulation of noble houses,
politics and legacy.

You do not play a country. You play a person inside a dynasty. Territory is a
consequence of good characters, not the objective itself.

The setting is high-medieval with a light fantasy overlay: multiple distinct
cultures and multiple religions, so cultural and religious identity diverge
rather than being cosmetic labels.

The question the game asks, repeatedly: can this character hold onto power,
and what will it cost them?

Two commitments follow from this and shape every system below.

  MANY VALID PLAYSTYLES. There is no single correct way to play. See
  section 4.

  A WORLD THE PLAYER SHAPES. Cultures, religions and their content are
  authored by the player and generated to order, so the game can become any
  setting. See 6.20 and 6.21.

The first paragraph above is DRAFT - inferred from the existing implementation.
The two commitments are designer-set.

================================================================================
2. DESIGN PILLARS
================================================================================

Every design decision is measured against these. If a proposal contradicts a
pillar, the pillar wins or the pillar gets revised deliberately.

PILLAR 1 - CHARACTERS ARE THE CONTENT
  A specific ruler with specific stats, traits, relationships and opinions is
  more interesting than a map with more detail. Systems serve character drama
  first. If a system can be expressed as "a number on the map" instead of
  "something a person wants or fears", prefer the person.

PILLAR 2 - TIME IS THE CURRENCY
  Nothing is instantaneous. Actions take days, council roles run for weeks,
  opinions accrue over years. Progress feels earned because it is slow. Any
  new system must declare what it costs in days and must be worth that wait.

PILLAR 3 - PEOPLE, NOT NUMBERS, HOLD POWER
  Authority flows through councils, families, marriages and opinion. The
  player is frequently dependent on other people liking them. Systems that let
  the player bypass other characters work against this pillar.

PILLAR 4 - CONSEQUENCE OVER OPTIMISATION
  Failure should hurt in interesting, legible, persistent ways. Not a
  subtraction from a total. A system that can be played optimally without ever
  taking a risk is not finished.

PILLAR 5 - LEGIBILITY
  The player can always understand why something happened. Hidden math,
  invisible weighting and unexplained outcomes all violate this. If the
  player cannot say "I did X and got Y", the system is not done.

PILLAR 6 - MANY VALID PLAYSTYLES
  The game has no single correct way to play. Materially different paths -
  landless adventurer, landed knight, empire-builder - must each be viable
  over a full campaign. Systems gate on REQUIREMENTS a character can meet,
  never on PERMISSION a character does not have. A king may go and explore
  caves. It will generally be a poor use of his time, and that is the correct
  and sufficient penalty.

PILLAR 7 - THE PLAYER SHAPES THE WORLD
  Cultures, religions and their content are schemas, not scripts. The game
  ships generic starting examples to give the world a face, and the player may
  replace or extend them to make the world anything they want. Where content
  is generated, it is generated the way terrain is: from parameters the player
  controls.

PILLARS 1-5 ARE DRAFT - inferred from the existing implementation, not yet
reviewed by the designer. Pillars 6 and 7 are designer-set.

================================================================================
3. THE DESIGN MODEL
================================================================================

What the game simulates, conceptually:

  PEOPLE        Characters with stats, traits, relationships, opinions,
                ambitions, titles, family. They age, they die, they inherit.

  PLACES        Locations with population, type, discovery state and
                improvements. Some are held, some are only visited.

  LAND          Terrain, provinces, regions, roads and rivers. Terrain decides
                what can exist where, and shapes how land divides up.

  STRUCTURE     Houses, cultures, religions, titles and vassalage. Who
                belongs to whom, and what they believe.

  TIME          A day clock. Everything is measured in days.

  PRESSURE      Events, opinions and needs that create problems to solve.

The intended emotional shape: you are managing a handful of overlapping
commitments that resolve on different timescales, and you cannot be everywhere.

================================================================================
4. PLAYSTYLES AND UNIVERSALITY
================================================================================

INTENT
  The game must not have a single correct way to play. At minimum these
  archetypes must each be viable over a full campaign:

    THE LANDLESS    No titles. Takes quests, explores ruins and caves, signs up
                    with institutions. Converts time into renown, and renown
                    into land.

    THE KNIGHT      Landed, serving in a ruler's army. Fights wars for renown,
                    land and dynasty. Martial and Combat Strength lead.

    THE RULER       Holds a realm. Councils, politics, legitimacy, peace.
                    Stewardship, Diplomacy and Learning lead.

  These are not separate modes or separate games. They are one system set
  approached from different starting positions.

THE RULE: REQUIREMENTS, NOT PERMISSION
  A legitimate gate states a property OF THE CHARACTER, or of their standing,
  that they either have or can obtain. An illegitimate gate states a ROLE the
  character occupies - "only rulers may do this", "only the player character
  may do this".

  Requirements are varied and there are many kinds. A gate may rest on:

    SKILL         A minimum in a stat. Combat Strength to lead a company.
    TRAIT         A quality of character. Brave, Greedy, Devout.
    CULTURE       Membership of a cultural group.
    RELIGION      Faith, or membership of a faith.
    RACE          Lineage or species.
    STANDING      Membership of an institution. A guild rank, a commission.
    TITLE         A rank or claim, where the rank is genuinely part of the task.
    RESOURCES     Wealth, equipment, a retinue, supplies.
    WORLD STATE   The state of the world. A location discovered, a war ended,
                  a season, a dynasty fallen.

  Consequence: the ruler who wants to spend a month in a cave is allowed to, if
  he meets the cave's requirements. The cost is opportunity, not refusal - he
  forfeits a month of council deployments, income, defence and events.

  This is why the day clock matters more than any lock. Pillar 2 and this
  pillar are the same mechanism: time is the cost, so any use of time is
  permitted, and the player judges whether it was worth it.

REQUIREMENTS FALL INTO TWO KINDS, AND THE DIFFERENCE MATTERS

  ATTAINABLE      The character can acquire it. Skills through growth, traits
                  through events, wealth through service, standing through
                  promotion, faith through conversion.

  IMMANENT        The character cannot acquire it. Race. Ancestry. Birth. A
                  closed guild. A bloodline.

  Both are legitimate requirements. Both gate actions honestly. But an
  imminent requirement cannot be crossed by effort, only by circumstance.

  THE DESIGN CONSEQUENCE: a world made mostly of attainable requirements is a
  world where any character can eventually do anything, and Pillar 6 holds. A
  world with many imminent requirements is a world where the set of things a
  character can ever do is fixed at birth, which is closer to a permadeath
  roguelite than a dynasty game.

  Therefore:
    - Attainable requirements should dominate where they can.
    - Imminent requirements should be scarce, and should carry weight when they
      do apply rather than forming a blanket of exclusion.
    - An imminent requirement should usually come with a path to compensating
      advantage elsewhere. A knight who cannot enter the scholars' guild should
      have something the scholars cannot do.

  This also settles the earlier claim about skill progression. Under attainable
  requirements, skill growth is what converts a gate from a wall into a goal.
  A closed door that opens as the character improves is a design feature; a
  closed door that never opens is a wall the player will route around or resent.

  A caution on identity requirements specifically. "Must be of this culture" and
  "must worship this god" are legitimate requirements, but they are easy to
  overuse as a substitute for design, and they carry a specific risk: if
  identity gates are the main way content is restricted, then the game is
  quietly telling the player their generated culture matters more than their
  choices. Identity should gate some things in some places, not be the default
  answer for why an action is unavailable.

THE CROSS-PLAYSTYLE CURRENCY
  Renown is intended to be the currency that makes all three paths
  interconnect:

    Adventurer   time -> renown
    Knight       war -> renown -> land
    Ruler        legitimacy -> renown -> stability

  If renown cannot be earned by a character holding no titles, the landless
  path is economically dead no matter how many quests exist.

INSTITUTIONS BEYOND THE STATE
  The adventurer path implies the game needs organisations that are not crowns.
  Guilds, mercenary companies, orders and fellowships give a landless
  character somewhere to belong, somewhere to take work from, and a ladder to
  climb. Houses and councils serve the landed paths; a third kind of
  institution serves the landless one.

OPEN QUESTIONS
  - What does a landless character call home, and how do they store wealth?
  - Are institutions ranked internally the way titles are, with their own
    seats, ranks and promotions?
  - Can a character move between archetypes mid-campaign?

5. HOW MUCH OF THE WORLD SIMULATES
================================================================================
The world is not a backdrop. Real characters live out real lives whether or not
the player is watching, because the alternative destroys the genre. If a world
exists only where the player looks, then the player's own importance is the only
thing being simulated.

  THE TWO EXTRESES, AND WHY NEITHER WORKS

    SIMULATE NOTHING OFF-SCREEN
      Cheap, and it quietly turns the game into a management sim. Every
      interesting person is one the player already found. The world becomes
      static scenery that decorates the player's own story. It also makes
      PERMANENT succession (6.6) hollow, because dying ends contact with a world
      that was never really running.

    SIMULATE EVERYTHING EQUALLY
      Alive, but expensive and unfair. Thousands of characters each running
      opinion, needs, actions and careers per tick will not scale. It also
      produces a specific failure: the player becomes an administrator of a
      spreadsheet, prioritising remote problems that will never matter.

  THE RESOLUTION: SIMULATE BY IMPORTANCE, NOT BY PRESENCE
    The distinction is between what is SIMULATED and what is RESOLVED.

      Not every character needs full simulation. Simulation is expensive, and
      most unlanded commoners lead simple lives with little that meaningfully
      affects the playable drama. To keep the world alive without drowning the
      player in administration, full simulation is reserved for characters of
      consequence: landed rulers and their courts, members of the player's
      dynasty and close kin, characters with active claims, rivals, or schemes
      involving the player, and a deliberately limited set of important
      characters (such as notable adventurers, guild leaders, or other figures
      the story has marked as significant).

      Unlanded commoners - the vast majority of the population - are not
      simulated day-by-day. They may age, marry, and produce children in
      aggregate over seasons or years, creating a plausible backdrop without
      generating individual actions for every peasant. Aggregated pools are
      purely statistical; notable individuals are not pre-generated - they are
      spawned at runtime when they become relevant. When a specific commoner
      becomes relevant (drawn into a quest, married into the court, becomes a
      claimant, etc.), they are promoted to full simulation with a plausible
      backstory generated at that moment, sized to fit their importance.

      Children are fully simulated from birth (as noted in 6.5), but they have
      very limited agency while young. A newborn or infant has little to do;
      their days consist mainly of growth, health, and relationships. Age 12 is
      the baseline threshold where children gain meaningful agency (full
      simulation actions). This may later become culture- or setting-defined.
      While under the agency threshold, children have limited scope: education,
      study, play, visit, and reacting to certain events. Routine political or
      military actions are not available. Education is their primary structured
      activity with bidirectional influence - both guardian and child preferences
      matter, gated by culture, standing, and guardianship. This preserves the
      dynasty's continuity without creating busywork for children.

      If a baby or minor inherits a title, a regency applies (see 6.6). The form
      and strictness of regency varies by culture, religion, and style of
      rulership - it is not universal. The heir remains simulated throughout
      their minority, but command rests with the regency council.

    Relevance determines whether a non-commoner is fully simulated. A character
    in a distant province the player has never visited may still be fully
    simulated because they are the player's mother, or married to the player's
    character, or the heir. Conversely, an unlanded stranger has no individual
    simulation regardless of proximity. The player's own attention and the
    character's importance to the story define the fidelity boundary, not the
    map alone.

  WHAT MUST NEVER BE AGGREGATED
    Four categories, or the illusion breaks:

      DYNASTY     The player's own bloodline, always. Children are fully
                  simulated from birth. They have very limited agency until
                  around age 12 (starting baseline; may be culture/setting-defined
                  later), mainly growing, staying healthy, building relationships,
                  and reacting to occasional events. Education is their structured
                  activity, with player influence varying by context. If the
                  player's heir grows, ages and dies on an abstracted clock,
                  succession is a cutscene rather than a fact.

      KIN AND CLOSE TIES
                  Anyone the player's opinion of matters to, and close family.
                  If a friend or close relation dies off-screen and the player
                  is told a decade later, that is a lie about a relationship.

      RIVALRY      Anyone with an active grudge, claim or scheme against the
                  player. Threats are the player's business.

      LANDED AND IMPORTANT
                  Landed characters, their courts, and any character marked as
                  important to the story (adventurers, guild leaders, notable
                  figures) must remain at sufficient fidelity to preserve
                  consequence.

  OPEN QUESTIONS
  - Budget ceiling: target the maximum number of fully simulated characters
    possible within performance constraints. Determine the exact ceiling through
    playtesting; it is not fixed in this document.
  - Aggregation granularity: implement settlement-level aggregation as the
    primary baseline for unlanded commoners. The system may adjust granularity
    if needed, but start at settlement level.
  - Promotion/demotion: demotion is not the default behavior and should be
    avoided. Characters are promoted when relevance demands it; if budget
    pressure arises, demotion is considered only as a last resort. Important
    figures (e.g. revolution leaders) may be spawned at key moments, persist
    through their arc, and may be retired/deleted at arc end - with possibility
    to save them for potential return. Exact thresholds are to be discovered
    through testing.
  - Adventurer cap: no fixed cap. Adventurers are an example of characters that
    cannot be aggregated and emerge naturally through story and events, not via
    competition for fixed slots. Discover practical limits through testing.
  - What marks someone as "important": in addition to landed, dynasty/close kin,
    rivals/claims, and schemes involving the player, the list may include
    guild/order leaders, renowned figures meeting a renown threshold,
    event-generated figures, patrons, and other story-driven characters. The
    "important figures" set will grow organically; caps/quotas are not fixed.
    Maximize simulation while staying within budget, trimming only when necessary
    based on testing.
  - How does relevance decay? Relevance does not decay automatically with time.
    It changes only through events, relationships, and story state (e.g. rivalries
    resolved, quests completed, claims extinguished). Demotion is rare; retention
    is preferred. Only when facing simulation pressure will demotion be considered.
    Arc-based figures may be retired/deleted at arc end with option to save for
    potential return. This preserves meaningful connections.
  - Should the player be able to see simulation edges? This needs clarification on
    consequences and UI - expose subtle indicators only if it doesn't break
    immersion, and respect the chosen reveal mode (see 6.25). Consider an
    optional setting.

LOOP
  Generate world -> acquire and hold territory -> characters age and change ->
  councils and events apply pressure -> you spend time to hold it together ->
  succession -> repeat with a new character

PACING MODEL  [LIVE]
  The clock is the game's fundamental constraint. New systems must fit it.

    1 in-game day per real second at normal speed
    Fast-forward is 3x
    Payday every 15 days
    Year passes every 360 days
    One year is roughly six real minutes

  Scale guidance for new systems:

    Actions / interactions ....... hours to a few days
    Council deployments .......... roughly 2-4 weeks
    Schemes and intrigue ......... weeks
    Wars .......................... months
    Reforms, schisms .............. years

  A system that resolves faster than a day has not been given a real cost. A
  system that resolves slower than a year will feel disconnected.

================================================================================
6. SYSTEMS
================================================================================

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.1 CHARACTERS                                          [PARTIAL]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A character has identity (name, age, gender, portrait), resources (wealth,
renown, piety), six skills (Diplomacy, Martial, Stewardship, Intrigue,
Learning, Combat Strength), traits, relationships, held titles, an optional
council seat, and family links.

INTENT
  Characters are the thing the player actually inhabits and cares about. They
  need to be individually legible and individually different.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Characters do not improve. Skill experience is awarded by actions but
    nothing converts it into growth. A character is fixed at creation except
    through traits and age. This directly undermines Pillar 1: there is no arc.
  - No ambition, wants or personality beyond traits.
  - Ageing is tick-based; mortality and inheritance are undesigned.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.2 SKILL PROGRESSION                                   [ABSENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  Skills must grow, slowly and conditionally, so a character has a trajectory
  and the player has something to invest in. Growth should be gated by
  circumstance (age, events, teaching, hardship), not just by spending a pool.

WHY THIS IS THE HIGHEST-PRIORITY ABSENT SYSTEM
  Systems gate on requirements rather than permission (section 4), and a skill
  threshold is the archetypal legitimate gate. But a gate on a stat that never
  changes is just a character-creation label.

  So skill growth is not a nice-to-have progression system. It is what makes
  requirement-gating mean anything, and therefore what makes Pillar 6 real.
  A knight who trains becomes able to do things a squire could not.

NO SPENDABLE POOL
  Growth is diegetic. There is no experience currency and no stat screen where
  the player allocates points. This is the correct choice for the game, and the
  reason matters: a spendable pool turns character development into a menu,
  which would put the player in the role of an administrator rather than a
  person making consequential choices with limited time.

  The player's lever is therefore WHICH experiences they pursue, not how many
  points they assign. A character sent to be a tutor grows Learning and
  Diplomacy. The same character sent to a levy grows Martial and Combat
  Strength. Both cost days, and days are the real currency (Pillar 2).

SOURCES OF GROWTH
  Four sources, all diegetic. Each should be legible to the player as an
  explanation for why a character is the way they are.

    INHERITANCE   Base stats derive partly from parents. This makes bloodline
                  meaningful and makes the dynasty a genuine asset: a house
                  that breeds for scholarship produces scholars for free, and
                  one that breeds for martial skill produces soldiers. It also
                  means a bad bloodline is a long-term problem, which is part
                  of what makes inheritance tense.

    TUTORS       Education during childhood and youth. Availability depends on
                  wealth, standing and culture, so a poor character may simply
                  not be taught. This is a primary way the world reproduces its
                  own hierarchy, and a legitimate target for the player to
                  break by finding a patron willing to sponsor an education.

    LIFE EVENTS   Specific things that happened. A siege, a betrayal, a
                  long illness, a year spent on campaign. These should be
                  individual and memorable, not generic per-day accrual. A
                  character who survived a siege should be recognisably
                  different from one who did not.

    TRAITS        Traits modulate and sometimes grant skills outright. Already
                  partially implemented - see 6.4.

WHAT THIS IMPLIES FOR REQUIREMENT GATES
  Because growth is slow, event-driven and partly inherited, a skill gate is a
  long-term goal rather than a menu choice. Two consequences to design for:

    - A gate must not require a stat so high that a normal character cannot
      reach it within a plausible lifetime. The gate should mark the edge of
      competence, not the edge of the possible.
    - Because inheritance feeds growth, requirements can become effectively
      hereditary. A house that cannot pass a Martial gate may never pass it.
      That is thematically right for a dynasty game, but it must be visible to
      the player early enough to plan around, or it reads as an unexplained
      wall.

LEGIBILITY OF GROWTH
  Growth is diegetic, so the player must be able to read it as narrative rather
  than as arithmetic. Each of the four sources should be visible as an
  explanation: this character was taught by a tutor, that one survived a siege,
  this one's father fought in three wars. Pillar 5 applies directly - if the
  player cannot say why a character is the way they are, the system is not done.

  Legibility carries exactly two obligations, and neither of them is prediction.

    EXPLAIN THE PAST    Every point of growth a character has is attributable to
                        a named source. The player can always answer "why is he a
                        9 in Martial" with "three campaigns and a siege".

    SHOW THE PRESENT GAP
                        For any requirement the character currently fails, the
                        shortfall is visible and named. This is the same
                        obligation as in 6.19 - show the shortfall, and let the
                        player see routes they do not yet qualify for. A closed
                        door with no stated distance is indistinguishable from a
                        wall.

  Projecting a future ceiling is explicitly not one of these obligations.
  Showing what a character could still become is a planning aid, and it is a
  secondary concern rather than a core one. It is also in tension with the four
  sources above: because growth is partly inherited and partly event-driven, a
  projected ceiling is a guess wearing the clothes of a fact. A number the
  player built a plan around, which then turns out to be wrong, is worse than no
  number at all.

OPEN QUESTIONS
  - Is there a hard cap by age, and do physical skills decay in old age?
  - Does a character choose who tutors them, or is it decided by their
    guardians?
  - How much of a parent's stats is inherited, and is it modified by the
    parent's own traits?
  - Can a character exceed the skill ceiling of both parents through
    circumstance alone?

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.3 OPINION AND RELATIONSHIPS                            [LIVE]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Opinion between two characters is a layered score, clamped to a fixed range,
built from: family bonds, political ties, shared identity (house, religion,
culture), trait agreement and opposition, diplomacy and renown differences,
and timed modifiers.

Timed opinion modifiers carry a duration in years and decay annually, so
favours and grudges fade over a lifetime.

INTENT
  This is the signature system and the engine of Pillar 3. Relationships should
  be the primary source of both opportunity and obstruction.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Opinion is calculated but not directly playable. There is no player-facing
    lever on someone else's opinion of you.
  - Relationships are a fixed type list. There is no way to create, change or
    destroy a relationship through play.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.4 TRAITS                                               [PARTIAL]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Traits are data assets carrying stat modifiers, an opinion value, and an
optional opposing trait reference. New characters are assigned a small number
of unique traits.

INTENT
  Traits are how characters differ from one another at a glance, and how the
  opinion system finds friction between people who would otherwise be
  compatible.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Content volume is very low. Only a handful of traits exist.
  - No trait acquisition, removal, or conflict between opposing traits on the
    same character.
  - No interaction with events.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.5 MARRIAGE, FAMILY AND DYNASTY                         [DESIGN]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  A dynasty game needs a way for dynasties to be MADE, not only to end. This is
  the section that creates houses, alliances, claims and heirs - the raw material
  every other system in section 6 consumes. Succession (6.6) describes what
  happens to an estate; this describes where an estate comes from.

  It is also the purest expression of Pillar 3. Nobody marries themselves into
  power. Every claim the player will ever hold began as two families deciding
  they needed each other, and the player is almost always outnumbered by the
  people whose agreement they need.

  Cultivating loyal branches should be rewarded, but not exploitable. Loyalty is
  contextual: kinship gives a foundation, but it must be earned and maintained
  over time through treatment, favors, titles, and shared history. It can erode
  if neglected or if interests diverge. This prevents a single 'favorite' from
  becoming an automatic yes-man dynasty.

MARRIAGE LAW IS A PROPERTY OF CULTURE AND FAITH
  There is no universal marriage rule in this game. Who may marry whom, how many
  spouses a character may hold, whether kin may marry, whether a spouse may hold
  office, and what it takes to dissolve a match - all of it belongs to the
  culture and faith involved, read through the capability-flag discipline in 6.21.

  The reason is the same reason 6.21 exists at all. A game that promises to be
  whatever the player wants cannot decide that marriage is monogamous and
  Christian in a world the player has generated as polytheistic and martial. If
  the mechanic needs to know whether a faith permits a cleric to marry, then
  CLERGY_CAN_MARRY is a property the faith has or does not have - not a constant
  in the marriage code.

  The consequence is that marriage becomes one of the most visible places where
  a generated world feels genuinely unlike a stock one. A culture that permits
  sibling marriage produces a different political graph from one that forbids it,
  because claims concentrate differently. A faith whose clergy may not marry
  produces religious characters whose marriages are dynastic arrangements rather
  than love matches, and that is a different game to play.

  Inbreeding is the other side of dynastic concentration. Marrying too closely
  carries compounding costs (health, fertility, negative traits). At the same time,
  stacking the same strong traits produces diminishing returns over generations
  (genetic drift), and good outside marriages come with political pressure and
  cost. This combination prevents exploitable 'super-soldier inbred' dynasties
  while still rewarding deliberate consolidation.

  These are IDENTITY gates in the section 4 sense, and the caution there applies
  with force: they should gate some things in some places, and be legible as
  culture rather than presented as arbitrary restriction. The player must be able
  to say "this is not permitted in our faith" and be right.

BOTH PARTIES MAY APPROACH
  Suits may be made by the player or received by the player. A character courts,
  proposes, negotiates and refuses; and equally, a character is courted,
  proposed to, and propositioned by someone they may not want.

  An unwanted suit is not a free accept. Being pursued is a social event with a
  cost either way - refusing a powerful house costs standing, and accepting a
  suit you do not want costs the days and the consequences of a marriage you did
  not choose. This is a direct application of section 4's rule that the cost of a
  choice is time and opportunity, never refusal: the game should let the player
  walk into a bad marriage and then live with it.

RELEVANCE BRINGS THEM TOGETHER
  Matches are proposed between characters who have a reason to meet, and the
  reason is relevance rather than proximity. The same principle governs diplomacy
  in 6.15 and the resolution tiers in section 5. A character is a plausible
  partner if there is a reason for the two houses to want an alliance - a
  border, a war, a claim, a debt, a shared enemy, or simply standing that would
  be gained by pooling it.

  Distance should matter, but as cost rather than as exclusion. A match between
  houses on opposite sides of the map takes months to arrange, which is a real
  price and never a refusal.

MARRIAGE IS AN OPINION TRANSACTION
  A marriage is the clearest conversion of opinion into power the game has. Two
  houses that dislike each other become, through a marriage, an alliance with
  shared interests - and two houses that already trust each other can extract far
  more from the same arrangement than two who do not.

  So the opinion of the relevant characters should be visible before, during and
  after a match is negotiated, and the player should be able to say why a
  marriage was possible and what it was worth. Pillar 5 applies with no
  exceptions here: if the player cannot explain why the match happened, the
  marriage system is not finished, however good the outcome was.

CHILDREN ARE SIMULATED FROM BIRTH
  Every child is a full character from the moment of birth: named, with traits,
  skills, opinions, a place in the family graph, and a mortality clock. They are
  never aggregated.

  This follows directly from section 5's rule that DYNASTY is never aggregated.
  The player's own bloodline is the one thing the simulation must never abstract,
  because if a child grows and dies on a coarse clock, succession stops being a
  fact and becomes a cutscene. A dynasty game whose heirs are statistics is a
  management game.

  Children of characters the player has never met may be aggregated in the
  ordinary way, and usually should be - a full character for every child in the
  world is exactly the budget problem section 5 leaves open.

WHAT A PARENT CONTROLS
  A parent can influence how a child grows, and the mechanisms available are
  themselves cultural and religious. Guardians decide a child's early rearing; a
  tutor decides what they are taught; a household's culture decides what is
  considered worth teaching at all.

  The deliberate answer is that all of these exist, and which are available
  depends on the setting - a culture that invests in scholarly tutors offers a
  different lever than one that puts children in the field at twelve. This is
  6.2's INHERITANCE and TUTORS sources arriving through 6.21's capability flags,
  and it is what lets a dynasty deliberately breed for a capability across
  generations rather than merely hoping for it.

CHILD MORTALITY IS THE POINT
  Children die. That single fact is what makes succession planning an act of
  anxiety rather than an act of arithmetic, and it is the strongest reason for a
  player to care about opinion, physicians, faith, and the regard of a spouse.

  It also does useful structural work. A large family is insurance, so producing
  heirs is a legitimate strategy rather than a numbers exercise - and because
  claims persist to the house (6.6), a house with many surviving children is a
  house with many ways to lose a succession contest.

  Longevity and child survival remain uncertain. Natural mortality forms a
  baseline; illness, wounds, and chance events can claim lives at any age. Care
  (physicians, faith-based care, environment) can improve odds, but never
  guarantees survival.

  This interacts with 6.24, and it should: the lethality of childhood illness is a
  setting property, so a generated world can be a place where dynasties are short
  and brutal or long and stable, and the player should be able to feel which.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - No marriage, family or dynastic relationship of any kind exists in code.
  - No children as characters. See 6.1, where ageing is tick-based and mortality
    and inheritance are undesigned.
  - None of the capability flags this depends on exist yet. See 6.21.

OPEN QUESTIONS
  - How are marriageable candidates surfaced? By rank, by relevance, by culture,
    or as a court that presents a shortlist?
  - Can a marriage be annulled or dissolved, and who may petition for it?
  - How many children does a character have, and is that a fixed roll or a
    function of age, health and the world?
  - Are the children of a disputed succession themselves contested? See 6.6.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.6 SUCCESSION AND INHERITANCE                           [DESIGN]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  Essential for a dynasty game. Succession is where characters, titles,
  debts, enemies and traits transfer between people. It should be the moment
  the player most dreads and most plans for.

SUCCESSION IS A WORLD-GENERATION CHOICE
  The player chooses a succession rule before the campaign begins, from a menu
  of modes. This is a setting, not a mechanic the player configures mid-run, so
  it belongs alongside the other generation parameters in 6.20 - chosen once,
  before the world exists, and governing the whole game.

  SCOPE: one mode first, then the others
    The first implementation is ANY CHARACTER: on death, play continues as any
    living character of the player's choosing. This is the loosest bond and the
    simplest to build, and it is a sufficient game on its own - it never forces
    the player into a bloodline they did not choose.

    The remaining modes are added afterwards as settings over the same underlying
    succession machinery. They should not require reworking character death,
    because the same system serves all of them: find living characters, present
    them, apply the chosen rule to pick one, transfer the estate.

    Designing the simple mode first is also the safer order. It exercises death,
    estate transfer and character selection with no extra rules on top, so any
    problems found there do not have to be untangled from five interacting modes.

  The modes, in increasing distance from the bloodline:

    ANY CHARACTER   Play continues as any living character, chosen by the
                    player. The loosest bond - you are a participant in a
                    world rather than the head of a house. Built first.

    CIRCLE          Play continues as a chosen friend, spouse, rival or
                    courtier rather than an heir. Succession becomes lateral:
                    you move through the social graph instead of down a family
                    tree.

    BLOODLINE       Play continues as the heir, chosen automatically by
                    succession rules. The dynasty experience: you are the
                    family, and the family outlives you.

    SELECT AN HEIR  On death the player chooses which heir to continue as. Adds
                    a real strategic decision each generation - the succession
                    crisis becomes a decision point rather than a cutscene.

    PERMANENT       The run ends when the player character dies. Highest
                    stakes. Every campaign is one life, so the game is a
                    sequence of short, intense lives rather than a dynasty.

    CIRCLE deserves a note. It means who you die next to matters more than who you
  are related to, which inverts the usual dynasty framing and leans entirely on
  relationships being legible. If relationships are opaque, this mode is
  unplayable.

  Selecting a mode should not exclude others from the simulation. Even under
  PERMANENT, the dynasty continues without the player - heirs rule, marry,
  age and die on their own. The world does not pause for the player's death.
  This is what makes permanent death meaningful rather than merely punishing.

WHAT PASSES TO AN HEIR
  Six categories, each with a settled position. The organising principle is that
  an heir inherits POSITIONS AND CLAIMS, but never the personal experience that
  made their parent's life legible.

    TITLES       Transfer, subject to the succession rule in force. A title held
                 by the deceased passes under that rule, and where the rule is
                 contested the contest is the interesting part - see the claims
                 note below.

    WEALTH       Transfers, but as an ESTATE rather than as cash. The heir
                 inherits obligations, income-earning holdings and standing,
                 not a balance. This keeps 6.11's "no expenses" problem from
                 being inherited as a problem: a sum of money with no cost
                 attached to it is not wealth, it is a number.

    RENOWN       Does NOT transfer as personal renown. It settles into HOUSE
                 STANDING instead, which acts as a floor beneath the heir's own
                 renown and colours how others initially treat them, but which
                 the heir cannot spend as their own reputation.

                 The reasoning matters, because section 4 makes renown the
                 cross-playstyle currency and 6.19 makes it the resource that
                 buys land. A dynasty must be able to accumulate standing across
                 generations or the long game has no long arc. But if renown
                 transferred intact, the dynasty game degenerates into a
                 reputation stack that one fortunate ancestor maxes out, and
                 every subsequent generation plays no part in it. Splitting it -
                 standing persists, personal renown must be re-earned - is what
                 makes inheritance a head start rather than a finished
                 character.

    PIETY        Does not transfer as the heir's own piety. It converts into an
                 OBLIGATION attached to the standing that came with the
                 titles - a debt owed to a faith or an institution, with terms.
                 This makes religion a route to power (6.19) rather than a
                 score, and it gives a hostile creditor something to hold over
                 a new ruler.

    CLAIMS       Persist to the HOUSE, not to the individual. A claim is a
                 statement about the dynasty's relationship to a title, so it
                 outlives the character who can press it. This is why a dynasty
                 can lose a war it was not alive to start, and why rivals must
                 be permanently eliminated rather than merely defeated once.

    RELATIONSHIPS Do not transfer, in any form, and this is the strictest rule
                 in the section. An heir who inherits their parent's friends is
                 the fastest available way to make 6.3 meaningless, because
                 opinion would become a property of the estate rather than
                 something between two people. Every relationship the heir has
                 on the day of succession is one they built or must earn.

  The consequence worth stating plainly: succession transfers leverage, not
  friendship. A new character begins with titles, wealth, standing, claims and
  debts, and begins with an empty relationship graph and no reputation they did
  not earn. That is a deliberately uncomfortable position, and it is the
  position the rest of the document is built to make playable.

MINORITY AND REGENCY
  A character who inherits before adulthood is ruled by a REGENCY COUNCIL until
  they come of age. The heir is playable throughout - they age, they learn, they
  have opinions and relationships - but they do not command. Council seats,
  deployments and war are held by the regency, not by them.

   If no regent is explicitly designated by the predecessor, the default regent
   is determined by culture and religion (data-driven). Regency is contestable -
   eligibility to contest is determined by culture and religion. The definition
   and strictness of regency vary by culture and religion and should be encoded
   as minimal data flags (covering key powers/restrictions). The age of majority
   is culture-defined.

  This is the direct expression of Pillar 3 and of the requirement rule in
  section 4. The minority is not a cutscene before the real game; it is a
  situation with the same shape as every other situation in the game, in which a
  young character's ability to act depends entirely on how other people regard
  them. A regent who likes the heir governs differently from a regent who fears
  them, and neither is chosen by the heir.

  It follows that a long minority under a hostile regency is one of the
  strongest tension sources available in this game, and that the design should
  protect it rather than smooth it away. Nothing about it should resolve
  quietly while the heir is off-screen.

OPEN QUESTIONS
  - Is succession resolved by character stats and opinion, or by fixed rules?
    A hybrid is likely: claims by right, contested by strength and standing.
  - How do multiple claimants and claimants' wars work? Note that this is now
    load-bearing rather than incidental - claims persist to the house, so a
    defeated claimant is not a solved problem.
  - Under CIRCLE, what happens when the character you have been playing dies
    and no one you like is alive?

WHAT IS MISSING
  - All of it. No death, no estate transfer, no regency, no claims exist in
    code. What is described above is a specification, not an implementation.
    See 6.22 for what the absence of a failure state currently means in play.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.7 COUNCILS                                            [LIVE]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A ruler has a council with a number of seats equal to their title rank. Five
roles exist, each bound to a skill: spymaster, diplomat, court priest,
marshall, steward. Each role offers a timed deployment action that sends the
councilor into the world with a map indicator. Vacant seats are visible, and
candidates are searched from court, family and realm.

INTENT
  Councils are the player's main engine of ongoing effect, and the clearest
  expression of Pillar 3 - power is exercised through other people.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Every role has exactly one action, and it is generic. There is no
    development, no specialisation, no council tension.
  - Councilors have no opinion relationship with each other or with the ruler,
    so council composition has no social consequence.
  - No councilor death, loyalty, or ambition.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.8 LOCATIONS AND DISCOVERY                             [LIVE]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Locations are either settlements (castle, city, church) that are holdable and
pay tax, or zones and points of interest (ruin, cave, oasis, village, temple,
mine, camp, lake) that are action sites. Each has a discovery state of
unvisited, visited, explored, which gates available actions. Location types are
distributed by terrain. Holdings support levelled improvements.

INTENT
  The surface of a place and its secrets are different content. This is the
  exploration pillar: knowing a place is not the same as understanding it.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Discovery state advances only through repeated action, with no narrative.
  - Locations have no personality, no ruler, no politics. A city is a number.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.9 ACTIONS                                             [LIVE]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Actions are the core verb. An action declares valid location types, whether it
requires ruling, whether it repeats, which discovery states expose it, a time
cost, resource costs, skill requirements, trait and title requirements, rewards,
and risk. The framework validates, queues and resolves them.

INTENT
  Actions are where the game's systems meet the player directly. They should be
  the main way character traits and skills express themselves.

NOTE ON THE "REQUIRES RULING" FLAG
  Actions currently declare a boolean "requires ruler". Under section 4 this is a
  permission gate, not a requirement, and it is the clearest existing
  contradiction of Pillar 6. A king exploring a cave is not a case the framework
  can currently express.

  It should be replaced by requirement declarations the character satisfies or
  does not. The set of requirement kinds is given in section 4 and must not be
  reduced to skills: skill, trait, culture, religion, race, standing, title,
  resources, and world state are all legitimate. A ruler who meets a cave's
  requirements should be able to go, and simply lose a month.

  The flag is not merely to be removed. It marks where the whole action
  vocabulary needs requirements rather than permissions.

  Note that requirements need not be character properties at all. "The location
  must be Explored", "it must not be winter", "this dynasty must no longer
  exist" are all legitimate gates, and several are more interesting than any
  stat threshold. World-state requirements are currently underrepresented in the
  action framework, which treats requirements almost entirely as character
  properties.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Actions are uniform. They resolve the same way regardless of who performs
    them or where.
  - Reward and risk are flat, with no narrative outcomes.
  - Actions are permanent per-location in effect; there is little persistent
    world change from performing one.
  - There is no action available to a character with no titles at all, which is
    why the landless path has nothing to do. See 6.19.
  - Requirements are modelled almost entirely as character properties. Culture,
    religion, race and world state should gate some actions; today they gate
    none. Identity gates are legitimate per section 4, but must not become the
    default excuse for why an action is unavailable.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.10 RACE AND LINEAGE                                   [ABSENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A gate may rest on race. The codebase has no concept of one, so this is new
content rather than an extension of an existing system.

INTENT
  Race should exist as a data property that requirements and events can read,
  not as a hardcoded assumption about the setting. A game that must support
  "whatever setting the player wants" cannot assume a human default.

WHY IT IS A REQUIREMENT AND NOT AN OPTIONAL EXTRA
  Race is the archetypal IMMANENT requirement (section 4) - it cannot be
  acquired by effort. That makes it a genuine design constraint rather than a
  flavour tag, and it has to be handled deliberately:
    - Its rarity as a gate. If race gates too much, capability is fixed at
      birth and Pillar 6 fails.
    - Its compensating advantages. What can a character of a given race do that
      others cannot? Without this, race is purely exclusionary.
    - Its interaction with generated cultures. Is race a property of culture, of
      world generation, or independent of both? See 6.20.

  Race is also the clearest test of the data-agnostic requirement in 6.21. If any system assumes a default
  human baseline, the any-setting promise is already broken.

OPEN QUESTIONS
  - Is race fixed at character generation, or can it change? A curse, a
    transformation, a cross-cultural marriage?
  - Is race a property of the individual, the house, or the culture?
  - Does a generated culture determine its races, or are races generated
    independently and then distributed among cultures?
  - At what realism setting does race stop being generated at all?
  - Can a character of an unusual race hold any title, or are titles gated by
    race? Note this is where 6.19's landless path could be quietly closed.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.11 ECONOMY                                             [PARTIAL]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Payday-driven. Property income is the sum of tax across held locations, scaled
by Stewardship. Renown accrues from Stewardship plus a bonus per title held.
Piety accrues from Learning. Levy is capped by military strength and
regenerates daily.

INTENT
  Wealth is a means, not a score. It should enable action and be a source of
  vulnerability, not something to maximise.

NOTE ON RENOWN AS THE BLOCKING ECONOMIC PROBLEM
  Renown currently accrues from Stewardship plus a bonus per TITLE HELD. A
  character with no titles therefore earns almost no renown, and cannot convert
  time into political capital.

  This makes the landless path economically dead. Renown must be
  earnable without land - from service, contracts, combat, exploration and
  deeds. It is the currency that lets the adventurer, the knight and the ruler
  reach the same social tier by different routes.

  Until this is resolved, adding guilds and quests gives a landless character
  things to do but no way to become anything.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - No expenses. Wealth only goes up, so there is no economic pressure.
  - No trade, no supply, no scarcity.
  - No financial events.
  - Renown has no non-landed sources. See the note above and 6.19.
  - No landless income of any kind: no wages, no service pay, no contract
    rewards.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.12 RANDOM EVENTS                                      [PARTIAL]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Events roll daily against a mean-time-to-happen, are scoped to a holding,
location or travel, have a global cooldown, and present as a popup with multiple
choices. Some events are modified by character state. Seven preset events exist.

INTENT
  Events are the game's pressure system and its narrative delivery. They are
  how the world acts on the player when the player is not acting.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Event AI does not choose. Characters always pick the first option.
  - Content volume is low.
  - No event chains, no follow-ups, no scheduled consequences.
  - Events do not interact with traits, cultures or religions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.13 TRAVEL                                             [PARTIAL]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Characters travel physically. Routes prefer roads, fall back to A* across the
cell graph, and impassable terrain blocks movement. Pawns walk the map. ETA is
shown. Multiple pawns can travel as a formation.

INTENT
  Distance is a real cost. Travel is what stops the map from being a menu.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Travel has no content attached to it beyond arrival. What happens on the
    road is only event-driven.
  - No interception, no meeting others in transit, no scouting.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.14 CULTURE, RELIGION, HOUSES                           [PARTIAL]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Houses are dynasties with a name, colour and members. Cultures and religions
carry compatibility and relation scoring that feeds opinion. Both carry
structures for heresy, schism, reform and holy war. A terrain affinity matrix
exists defining which cultures and religions are drawn to which terrain.

INTENT
  Identity must be something that changes behaviour and opinion, not a label.
  Culture and religion should make some things easy that are otherwise hard,
  and forbid others.

THIS IS THE SEED OF SOMETHING MUCH LARGER
  A culture with a naming convention, values, martial tradition and compatibility
  model is a general-purpose identity object. It would then also serve houses,
  institutions and generated content, which is how Pillar 7 becomes true without
  special-casing anything.

  The work is specified in 6.20 (generation, including the realism slider) and
  6.21 (player authoring and any-setting support). This entry is the current
  partial state of what those sections describe.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Culture and religion are nearly inert. Compatibility only feeds opinion.
  - Culture does not gate or modify actions.
  - Generated names are not culture-flavoured, so identity is invisible in the
    fiction.
  - Terrain affinity is not driving generation.
  - Heresy, schism, reform and holy war are stubs.
  - No schema. A culture and a religion are each a name plus opinion buffers,
    so there is nothing for a player to edit or a generator to fill in. This is
    the root limitation - See 6.21.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.15 DIPLOMACY                                           [DESIGN]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  Diplomacy is how characters who are not in the same room nonetheless come to
  terms, and it is a system every character has, not a privilege the player
  holds. Realms are not the actors - CHARACTERS are, including rulers, and their
  dealings happen whether or not the player is watching.

EVERY CHARACTER DIPLOMATES, AT DIFFERENT RESOLUTION
  The load-bearing decision here is that diplomacy is not a player-only system
  with an AI decoration. Every character in the world can open negotiations with
  every other character they have a reason to deal with, and the player's
  diplomatic options are simply their character's, exercised at full resolution.

  The difference is fidelity, not capability, and it follows section 5 exactly. An
  AI pair of characters negotiates in coarse steps at whatever resolution their
  relevance justifies - a season at a time, reaching a statistically reasonable
  outcome. The same negotiation between two characters the player is involved in,
  or kin to, resolves day by day with every option visible.

  The consequence is worth stating plainly, because it is unusual: there is no
  separate diplomacy AI to write. There is one negotiation system, and relevance
  decides how often it runs and how much of it the player sees. A player who
  watches closely does not get a different game - they get a better-resolution
  version of the same game everyone else is playing.

RELEVANCE BRINGS TWO CHARACTERS TO THE TABLE
  Characters negotiate when there is a reason for them to need each other. The
  reasons are the substance of the system, and they are the same reasons found in
  any real negotiation:

    A CLAIM OR TITLE      Someone believes they are owed something.
    A BORDER              Two holdings touch, and both want the edge.
    A WAR                 An alliance, a truce, a subsidy, a peace.
    A DEBT                Money owed, and the ability to refuse a loan.
    A FAITH OR CULTURE    Shared identity, or a rivalry sharpened by it.
    KINSHIP               Marriage, inheritance, a hostage, a vendetta.
    ACCESS                Trade routes, a port, a mountain pass, a ford.

  A pair with none of these does not negotiate, and the player should never see
  them try. This is the same relevance rule as section 5 and as marriage in 6.5,
  applied to negotiation - and it is what keeps the diplomacy layer affordable
  without starving the player of it.

THE WORLD COMES TO THE PLAYER
  Approaches arrive unprompted. Envoys present offers, refusals, provocations and
  invitations without the player having gone looking, and - importantly -
  IGNORING an approach is itself an answer with a cost. A neglected suitor, an
  unanswered insult or an expired treaty is a decision, and the world should treat
  it as one.

  This matters because it is what stops diplomacy from being a menu the player
  opens when they feel like it. Under Pillar 2 the player's attention is already
  the scarce resource; making them seek out every negotiation would mean the
  world only exists while they look at it, which section 5 identifies as the
  failure mode of simulating nothing off-screen.

  What the player can still do is refuse to be available - by being away, at
  war, or on a long errand. That is a way of paying in days to avoid a
  conversation, which is precisely the section 4 trade.

BREAKING AN AGREEMENT
  An agreement is not a flag that is set and never queried. Breaking one costs
  opinion with everyone who knew about it, which is more expensive the more
  parties and witnesses the agreement had - and that difference is the whole
  design. A private understanding broken quietly is cheap; a public treaty broken
  before two realms is a catastrophe.

  This is Pillar 4 working through opinion rather than through a subtraction. The
  player is not fined for betrayal, they are simply never trusted again by the
  people who watched.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Nothing. Vassalage exists as a title relationship and an opinion modifier,
    and there is no diplomacy layer at all - no marriages, alliances, tributes,
    non-aggression pacts or wars between realms.
  - There is no negotiation system for any character, AI or player.

OPEN QUESTIONS
  - What are the negotiable instruments? Alliances, truces, tributes,
    non-aggression, access rights, marriage contracts, dynastic pacts - and are
    they one system with different terms, or separate actions?
  - How long does a negotiation take, and can it be interrupted by events?
  - Do AI characters negotiate with each other in ways the player can learn about
    and exploit, or is the result opaque until it affects them?

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.16 INTRIGUE AND SCHEMES                                [DESIGN]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  Intrigue is the slow, deniable alternative to acting openly. It should let a
  character reach an outcome they could not reach legitimately, at the price of
  months, risk of discovery, and permanent damage to their standing if it goes
  wrong. Intrigue is a skill with nothing to spend it on today; this is what it
  spends it on.

LEVERAGE IS THE RESOURCE
  The organising decision is that there is one currency in this system and it is
  LEVERAGE - what you know about whom, and how much it would cost them for it to
  be known.

  Leverage accumulates passively from being a person's friend, servant, rival,
  creditor, physician or lover. It is not spent to be gathered; it is spent to be
  used. This is the reconciling decision between the three models that were in
  play: secrets and leverage are the resource, schemes are what you spend them
  on, and agents are simply the mechanism by which leverage moves.

WHAT LEVERAGE BUYS
  Schemes are attempts to convert leverage into an outcome. They should differ by
  what they attempt and how deniable they are, and they should always cost days
  that are longer than a council deployment:

    COMPROMISE   Learn something damaging that a target would pay to keep quiet.
    BLACKMAIL   Convert a compromise into money, a post, or a favour.
    SEDUCE       Bind someone to you through a relationship rather than a secret.
    SPY         Learn what leverage does not yet cover. Raises the rate at which
                other schemes are possible.
    POISON       Remove a rival quietly. The least deniable of the set.
    ASSASSINATE  Remove a rival definitively. Slower, deadlier, and ruinous if
                you are caught.
    FORGE        Manufacture a claim, a document or a lineage. The rarest and
                most destructive to attempt.

  The ordering is not decoration. A character needs SPY before they can build
  leverage worth blackmailing, and FORGE should be so expensive and so
  deniability-destroying that attempting it is a decision with a dynasty attached.
  That is the section 4 shape: each gate is a requirement the character can
  acquire, and acquiring it takes a life.

DISCOVERY IS THE COST OF ACTING
  A scheme is discovered by suspicion, by witnesses, and by the target
  counter-scheming - a target who suspects they are being schemed against can
  scheme back, which is where Intrigue-as-a-skill meets Intrigue-as-a-defence.

  Discovery is not a single roll. It accrues over the weeks the scheme runs, so a
  long scheme is riskier than a short one purely through exposure. That makes the
  time cost of Pillar 2 and the risk of the scheme the same dial, which is the
  most elegant thing this system can do and should not be given up lightly.

  Being discovered is not a failure state - see 6.22. It is a consequence: the
  target learns, opinion collapses across everyone who hears about it, and the
  player continues as a character who has been caught. The worst outcomes in this
  system should be survivable and permanent, not fatal.

LEGIBILITY
  Pillar 5 applies here more than anywhere else in the document, because intrigue
  is where a player is most likely to lose track of cause and effect. The player
  must always be able to answer: who suspects me, who is protecting me, and what
  exactly do I have on this person.

  That means suspicion should be visible as a number rather than inferred from
  behaviour, and leverage should be listed per target with its source. A scheme
  the player cannot trace back to the secret that enabled it is the one failure
  mode this system cannot have.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Intrigue is a skill with nothing to spend it on. No leverage, no schemes,
    no discovery, no suspicion.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.17 ARMIES AND WAR                                     [ABSENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Levies can be raised and travel as formations. What happens on arrival is not
designed.

INTENT
  Force is the ultimate expression of a claim. War should be rare, decisive and
  expensive, and it should be a political event as much as a battle.

WAR RESOLVES IN TWO LAYERS
  Not one system but two, at different timescales. This keeps war expensive in
  days without requiring the player to fight battles, and it means most of a war
  is spent on the part that produces consequences.

    THE CAMPAIGN    Months. Marches, sieges, supply, morale, attrition. This is
                    where a war is actually played. A commander who besieges
                    well wins without a battle ever being fought. Attrition,
                    supply and season should be able to destroy an army that
                    never lost a fight.

    THE CLASH       An instant. When two armies do meet, the result resolves as
                    an outcome weighted by force, the commander's Martial and
                    Combat Strength, and terrain. One roll, narrated. No tactical
                    mini-game.

  The consequence is that the player's skill lies in the campaign layer -
  choosing when to fight, where to position, how to supply - rather than in
  battle micro-management. That fits Pillar 1: war is about the competence of a
  character, not the reflexes of a player.

  It also means a war is measured in months against a council deployment's
  weeks and an action's days (section 5). War is the largest thing a character
  can do, and it should cost the largest amount of time.

COMMAND IS A REQUIREMENT, NOT A ROLE
  Under section 4, leading an army should be gated on capability - Combat
  Strength, Martial, possibly relevant traits - not on holding a title. A
  landless knight with the right skills can lead a company. This is exactly the
  kind of gate that only works if skills grow (6.2), so the two systems are
  coupled.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Arrival does nothing. Armies travel and then the existing code stops.
  - No campaign layer at all - no supply, morale, attrition or season.
  - No clash resolution.
  - No casualties, and therefore no consequence for the player character beyond
    the war's outcome. A character who wins a battle should be marked by it.
  - No peace terms. Winning a war is the easy half; what the victor actually
    gains is undecided. War must be costly in time, resources, and manpower.
    Campaign outcomes (attrition, supply) should be able to decide wars without
    battles. Peace terms need to reflect campaign results and be decided by
    the relevant actors. Wounded characters should have recovery paths with
    risk of permanent effects or career changes.

6.18 QUESTS                                               [DESIGN]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  Quests are how a character acquires a direction. Without them the player is a
  manager optimising holdings, which is the thing Pillar 1 exists to prevent.
  A quest is a want with a deadline, and deadlines are what make a character
  move.

TWO KINDS, AND THE DISTINCTION MATTERS
  Both kinds exist, and they are not variations on one another. Mixing them
  produces the same mush as mixing generated and authored cultures.

    GENERATED     Small, specific, and built from live world state: an unclaimed
                  ruin, a disputed inheritance, a bandit camp blocking a road,
                  a relic in a temple nobody has entered, a village asking for
                  help. Infinitely varied, because every one points at
                  something that actually exists in the world.

    AUTHORED      Long, hand-written, character-specific ARCS. A vendetta that
                  runs across a decade. A love story that crosses a war. These
                  carry the emotional weight that a generated objective cannot,
                  because they are about specific people the player has come to
                  know.

  The relationship between them is that authored arcs GENERATE the circumstances
  that generated quests appear in, and completed quests can feed authored arcs.
  A generated quest is how the player gets pulled into a village's problem; an
  authored arc is what happens if they pull hard enough.

  Generated quests are also the primary income and purpose source for the landless
  path. See 6.19 - a landless character has no holdings to optimise, so quests
  are not a secondary content source for them, they are the entire career.

QUEST SCOPE
  Quests attach to CHARACTERS, not to dynasties. A character's quest should end
  with that character - died, completed, abandoned, or carried to an unhappy end -
  while the dynasty's business is titles, claims and standing in 6.6.

  This keeps the two systems from blurring. A quest is about what a person wants;
  succession is about what a family keeps.

TIME IS THE DEADLINE
  Quests are the sharpest consumer of Pillar 2, and they should be the mechanism
  that makes weeks and months feel like real units. A quest with a six-month
  window that the player spends on a siege instead has lost it, and losing it
  should be a real outcome the player can live with - because 6.22 means there is
  no failure state, only a character who did not get what they wanted.

OPEN QUESTIONS
  - How many quests may a character hold at once? More than one is more
    interesting, since it creates the section 3 feeling of competing
    commitments, but it needs a hard cost in attention.
  - Are generated quests generated per character, or generated by the world and
    claimed by whoever is present?
  - Do quests resolve as outcomes, or do they chain into further quests?

WHAT IS MISSING
  - All of it. No quests, no deadlines, no character-scoped goals.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6.19 LANDLESS, GUILD AND MILITARY PLAY                  [ABSENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  No single playstyle may be privileged. A character starts somewhere and
  moves between positions over their life. A favorite knight or promising
  individual should be able to grow into something lasting through investment -
  titles, education, land, and personal bonds. This investment must feel
  rewarding (building loyalty, influence, and a lasting dynasty branch) without
  becoming exploitative.

STARTING POSITIONS
  Landless    No titles. Survives on quest rewards, service, wages or patronage.
              No court, no council. Must earn Renown to acquire land.
  Knight      Landed minor noble. Serves a liege, fights his wars, gains land
              and renown through service. Martial and Combat Strength lead.
  Ruler       Holds a title. Council, court, diplomacy, governance.
              Stewardship, Diplomacy and Learning lead.
  Adventurer  Joins a guild, mercenary company or holy order. Takes contracts
              across the world. An institution rather than a title.

  These are starting positions, not classes. A landless adventurer who earns a
  county becomes a Ruler. A Ruler can be deposed and become landless. A Knight
  can rise to an empire.

INSTITUTIONS BESIDE THE CROWN
  The landless need somewhere to belong that is not a court. Guilds,
  mercenary companies, holy orders and martial fellowships should exist as
  organisations with their own ranks, reputation and perks.

  They give the landless path:
    - Income that does not depend on land
    - Quests and contracts only members receive
    - A social ladder orthogonal to titles
    - A claim on land once enough Renown is earned

  Houses and councils serve the landed paths. This third kind of institution
  serves the landless one. See section 4.

HOW A CHARACTER ACQUIRES LAND
  There is no single route. Every route below is viable, and a character's
  circumstances determine which are open to them.

    CONQUEST        Take it in war. Favours the knight. Needs an army, a war,
                    and a claim that can be pressed.

    PURCHASE        Buy it outright. Favours the wealthy adventurer. Needs
                    accumulated wealth and a willing seller.

    GRANT           Be granted it by a liege, house or patron. Favours the loyal
                    and the well-liked - this route runs directly through the
                    opinion system.

    INSTITUTION     Earn it from rank in a guild, order or company. Favours the
                    institution player. The order owns land and grants it.

    MARRIAGE        Marry into it. The classic route. Needs a match, which needs
                    standing and usually an attractive trait.

    INHERITANCE     Receive it from a relative. Needs blood and their death.

    THE FAITH       Be granted it by a religious institution for service or
                    piety. This makes religion a route to power rather than
                    only a source of opinion, which is what makes a generated
                    religion matter mechanically.

    PETITION        Persuade a ruler to enfeoff you, by argument, gifts or
                    reputation. This is the route that makes Diplomacy and
                    Renown into land.

  The important consequence of having many routes is that they are NOT
  interchangeable. Each converts a different resource into land - an army,
  money, a patron's affection, institutional rank, a marriage, blood, piety,
  or reputation. A player who is strong on exactly one of these has a path,
  and a player strong on none of them is in real trouble.

  This is what prevents the landless path from being a slower version of the
  landed path. The landed character already has land and needs to defend or
  acquire more of it. The landless character must first convert some other
  resource into standing, and which resource depends on what they are good at.

ROUTES MUST EXIST, AND MUST BE SURFACED
  Two separate obligations, and it is worth keeping them apart.

  FIRST, THE ROUTE MUST EXIST AS AN OPTION AT ALL. This is a systems requirement.
  It is entirely possible to build a land acquisition system that only offers
  conquest and purchase, and to believe the other six routes are covered because
  the player can theoretically end up with land. That would be wrong. Each route
  on the list above should be a real, distinct mechanism that a character can
  pursue. If a route is only reachable by a scripted event or a very long chain,
  it is not a route.

  SECOND, THE ROUTE MUST BE VISIBLE WHEN THE CHARACTER COULD USE IT. This is
  mostly a UI and tutorial problem, not a systems problem - and it is not a
  design requirement in itself. The mechanisms are:

    - Surface a route when the character first becomes plausibly eligible. The
      player should not have to already know to ask.
    - Name the resource. A route that says "you are well regarded enough to be
      offered land" communicates what it wants far better than "petition
      available".
    - Show the shortfall. If a character is one Diplomacy short or lacks the
      renown, say so. A visible gap teaches; a hidden requirement does not.
    - Let players see routes they do not currently qualify for. Knowing
      petition exists and that they need reputation is more useful than a
      locked icon with no explanation.

  The design concern behind it is narrow: a player who never learns any route
  concludes the landless path is impossible, and if they conclude that early it
  colours how they read the whole game. That is a risk worth mitigating, but it
  is mitigated by presentation rather than by changing the systems.

CORE SYSTEMS MUST DEGRADE GRACEFULLY
  Council, court and governance currently assume a titled character with a
  court. Those systems should degrade rather than be unavailable for a landless
  character. A landless character can hold a council position under a patron,
  and can own wealth without owning land.

  A character with no titles is a first-class state (See 6.19), so "every character holds a title" is false and must not leak into
  core logic. See 6.11 - renown income currently depends on titles held.

OPEN QUESTIONS
  - What does a landless character call home, and how do they store wealth?
  - Are institutions ranked internally the way titles are, with their own seats
    and promotions?
  - Can a character move between archetypes mid-campaign, and what does it
    cost?

6.20 PROCEDURALLY GENERATED CULTURE AND RELIGION       [DESIGN]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  Culture and religion should be GENERATED at world creation, the same way
  terrain is generated, not authored as a fixed list. Every playthrough should
  have its own beliefs, its own peoples, and its own incompatibilities.

  The player does not author these from scratch. The player sets PARAMETERS and
  the world builds itself. This is the same principle as terrain generation
  applied to culture and faith.

WHY GENERATED RATHER THAN AUTHORED
  A fixed roster of four cultures and four religions cannot produce genuine
  variety across dozens of playthroughs. Generated cultures create organic
  opinion structures, because compatibility becomes an emergent property of
  what was generated rather than a fixed table.

THE GENERATION PARAMETERS
  These are the sliders the player controls before world creation.

    Realism      How closely generated cultures and religions resemble real-world
                 analogues. At one extreme, generate recognisable analogues of
                 real cultures and real historical faiths. At the other,
                 generate arbitrary abstract cultures and belief systems. This
                 is a continuous slider, not a preset.

    Diversity    How many distinct cultures and religions exist.

    Hostility    How strongly incompatible the generated groups are. Low values
                 produce a placid world where most groups get along; high values
                 produce a world where ancient grudges are baked in at creation.

    Dogma        How doctrinal the generated faiths are. Rigid religions generate
                 more heresy and schism; permissive ones generate more syncretism.

    Naming       How cultures name themselves, their gods, their holy places.
                 Real-world linguistic patterns, invented systems, or hybrid.

    Race         How many distinct races or lineages exist, how they are
                 distributed among cultures, and how visible they are. At the low
                 end, one human-like baseline. At the high end, many distinct
                 peoples with their own identities. See 6.10 - race is the
                 archetypal imminent requirement (section 4), so this parameter
                 controls how much of the world is fixed at birth.

    Start Era    Where the world begins. A high-medieval world, an early
                 industrial one, a post-collapse one. This is the parameter
                 that decides which content is even relevant, and it therefore
                 has to be set before anything else is generated.

    Technology   How much of the material world works, and what is assumed to
                 have been discovered. See 6.21 - this is one of the two
                 parameters that decides whether the result is high fantasy,
                 low fantasy, or neither.

    Magic        Whether magic exists, how common it is, whether it is ordinary
                 or shameful, and whether anyone knows how it works. Coupled to
                 Technology rather than independent of it, because a world that
                 has steam power generally has opinions about what magic is for.

    Succession   How the player continues when a character dies or retires.
                 Bloodline, permanent death, selected heir, any character, or
                 any member of the circle. This governs the shape of an entire
                 campaign, so it is chosen once at world creation alongside the
                 other parameters rather than configured mid-run. See 6.6.

  This is the complete set. Any system that wants to vary the world at creation
  time does so by reading one of these, not by inventing a setting of its own -
  see 6.21, which is the layer that depends on this one.

  These parameters are all in service of one goal: a world that is coherent with
  itself and different every time. A world where the realism slider is low, races
  are numerous, faiths are hostile and succession is permanent should feel like a
  recognisably different game from a placid, single-culture, bloodline world -
  without any of those differences requiring separate content.

  The same parameters should also drive how faithfuls and cultures relate to
  TERRAIN, since the terrain affinity system already exists. Setting a desert
  culture as expansionist and mountain-favouring should be an input to world
  generation, not a fixed matrix.

  Race, culture and religion are generated as three interacting layers rather
  than independently. A generation pass should produce races, then distribute
  them unevenly across cultures, then derive cultures' beliefs, values and
  compatibility from that distribution. Generating them independently and
  filtering afterwards would produce worlds that feel arbitrary.

TERRITIAL AFFINITY AS AN OUTPUT, NOT A TABLE
  Terrain affinity should be generated per playthrough. A culture's affinity
  for terrain should follow from its generated parameters, so it is internally
  consistent with the rest of what was generated.

OVERRIDES AND CUSTOM CONTENT
  Generation must never be the only option. The player should be able to:
    - Tweak any single generated culture or religion after generation
    - Replace any generated group entirely with a hand-authored one
    - Add custom groups alongside generated ones

  The player's authored content must be indistinguishable from generated
  content once in the world. Nothing in the simulation should care which
  source a culture came from.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Everything. Culture and religion are currently a fixed list of four each,
    assigned randomly to provinces.
  - No generation parameters exist.
  - Terrain affinity is a fixed preset matrix and is never called.
  - No race layer at all. See 6.10.

6.21 ANY-SETTING CUSTOMISATION                          [DESIGN]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  It should be possible to turn Nobles & Knights into a different game - a
  different genre, tone, or setting - without touching code, by configuring
  the world.

  This is the strongest form of the vision: not just "medieval fantasy with
  four cultures" but "whatever the player wants to play".

THE CUSTOMISATION LAYERS
  1. GENERATION PARAMETERS    High-level sliders, listed in full in 6.20. Realism,
                              diversity, hostility, dogma, naming, race, start
                              era, technology, magic and succession. They set the
                              tone of the world before it exists.

  2. FAITH AND CULTURE EDITORS  Structured editors for tenets, doctrines,
                              practices, values and traditions. Player defines
                              the content; the simulation reads it as data.

  3. CONTENT PACKS            Importable sets of pre-authored cultures,
                              religions, traits and events. A player can load a
                              Roman-totalitarian pack, a Lovecraftian pack, or a
                              post-apocalyptic pack and have a coherent world.

  4. HARDCODED REALITY        Anything that assumes medieval Europe. This is the
                              layer that must be identified and eliminated for
                              the promise to hold.

EXAMPLES OF SETTINGS THIS SHOULD SUPPORT
  - High fantasy with multiple rival gods and genuine religious war
  - Low fantasy where gods are unproven and faith is politics
  - Post-apocalyptic where "cultures" are survivor communities and "religion"
    is improvised
  - A world with no organised religion at all
  - A strictly secular game where faith is purely a political instrument

THE SIMULATION MUST BE DATA-AGNOSTIC
  Every system that reads culture or religion must read them as data, never as
  a hardcoded assumption. This is the discipline that makes the promise real.

  Concretely: no system may check for a religion by name, or assume a faith has
  a clergy, or assume cultures have language-based naming. If a mechanic needs
  one of those, it should be a property the faith or culture either has or does
  not have.

FAITH IS OPTIONAL, NOT ASSUMED
  The examples above include a world with no organised religion at all, and a
  strictly secular game where faith is purely a political instrument. Both are
  commitments, and neither is keepable unless the simulation can run without a
  religion in the world at all.

  So faith is an OPTIONAL LAYER. A world may be generated with religions, or
  with none. Where faith exists it may be institutional, or it may be a set of
  uncoordinated local practices that nobody organises. These are different
  worlds, not one world with the interface switched off - and the difference has
  to be legible in play. A character in a world without clergy must have
  something to seek instead, and whatever that is must not be a cathedral.

CAPABILITY FLAGS
  The mechanism that makes the above work is a small set of yes/no properties
  that systems READ rather than ASSUME - the concrete form of the discipline
  stated directly above.

  Scope note: this is deliberately smaller than a full schema, which remains
  undecided. Capability flags alone are enough to retire the promise of a
  secular world. They are not enough to build a generator from, because a
  generator has nothing to put in the other fields.

  A faith's flags:

    HAS_CLERGY          Is there an organised priesthood? A faith with clergy
                        has somewhere to join, ranks to climb and a building to
                        pray in. A faith without has none of these, and every
                        institution that would serve a devout character must
                        simply not exist for them - which removes the whole
                        institutional path in 6.19 for such a character.
    HAS_HOLY_PLACES     Are there consecrated sites? Shrines, temples, relics.
    HAS_SCRIPTURE       Is there a text, and is it authoritative? Determines
                        whether doctrine is settled or continuously negotiated.
    CLERGY_CAN_MARRY    Whether the devout may hold office and be married at
                        once. The game must never decide this for itself.
    HAS_HERESY          Can a character leave, and is leaving dangerous? This
                        is what gives 6.14's heresy system something to act on.
                        A faith that tolerates dissent needs a different system
                        entirely, not a heresy system with the volume turned
                        down.
    HAS_SCHISM          Can it split into mutually unintelligible branches?
    ACCEPTS_CONVERSION  Can a character change faith, and at what cost? A faith
                        that forbids it is an IMMANENT requirement (section 4),
                        and therefore scarce by design rather than routine.

  A culture's flags:

    HAS_NAMING_CONVENTION
                        Whether this culture names things systematically at all.
                        See the Naming parameter in 6.20 - a culture without one
                        must not be handed a hundred names to generate.
    HAS_MARTIAL_TRADITION
                        Whether the culture has an organised way of fighting. A
                        culture without one cannot produce a knightly order,
                        which closes the military-institution route in 6.19 for
                        its characters.
    HAS_KINSHIP         Whether kinship is tracked as descent, as household, or
                        not at all. This determines what succession MEANS for a
                        character in 6.6 - a culture without descent has no
                        BLOODLINE mode available inside it.

  The last flag is the sharpest example of the whole principle, and is worth
  keeping in mind when writing any generator: HAS_KINSHIP does not vary a
  cosmetic detail. It determines whether one of the five succession modes in
  6.6 is a legal option in the generated world, and the generator must know that
  before it finishes.

  Every flag above is a property that is generated, defaulted or authored -
  never a value that code assumes. A system that wants to reward devotion asks
  HAS_CLERGY; it does not reach for a priest.

OPEN QUESTIONS
  - What is the full field list for a faith and a culture? Capability flags
    settle the secular-world promise, but generation, editors and content packs
    all need the rest of the fields, and nothing specifies them yet. This blocks
    6.20 and the editors.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - No content schema. Cultures and religions are name-and-opinion-buffers.
  - No player-facing editors of any kind.
  - Hardcoded medieval assumptions throughout the character, council, title,
    action and event systems.
  - No content pack loading.

6.22 FAILURE AND CONSEQUENCE                               [DESIGN]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  There is no loss state. The game has no "you lost" condition, no defeated
  screen, and no point at which a campaign is ended for having played badly. What
  a player can lose is everything they hold, and what follows that loss is
  another playable position rather than an ending.

WHAT ENDS A CAMPAIGN
  Only the succession mode in force (6.6), and only in one case:

    PERMANENT      The run ends when the player character dies. This is the
                   entire point of the mode, and it is the only ending the game
                   has.

    BLOODLINE      Never ends. Play continues as the heir, indefinitely.
    SELECT AN HEIR Never ends. Play continues as whichever heir is chosen.
    CIRCLE         Never ends. Play continues as someone who was close.
    ANY CHARACTER  Never ends. Play continues as any living character.

  A run that ends for any other reason is a bug. Deposition is not death.
  Losing a war is not death. Losing every title is not death.

WHY THERE IS NO LOSS STATE
  Pillar 4 requires that failure hurt in interesting, legible, persistent ways,
  and explicitly rejects failure as "a subtraction from a total". A run-ending
  loss state is precisely a subtraction from a total - it is the game checking a
  number and ending the session when the player fails to keep it above zero. It
  is also, structurally, a single-life design: it makes the campaign one run
  rather than a chain of them, which is the roguelite frame 6.6 argues against
  when it calls permanent death "closer to a permadeath roguelite".

  Decisively, it would punish exactly the behaviour the pillars reward. A player
  who presses a claim they cannot afford, or wages a war they cannot supply,
  and loses everything has made the correct Pillar 4 decision and should get to
  play the consequence. Ending the run punishes risk-taking with a game-over
  screen, which teaches the player not to take risks at all.

  6.19 already designs the landing. A Ruler "can be deposed and become landless",
  and landless is a first-class starting position with its own eight routes back
  to land. So the worst thing that can happen to a character is to be returned
  to the game's opening position - which the player has already played once and
  therefore already knows how to handle.

WHAT LOSING ACTUALLY COSTS
  Since nothing is subtracted from a total, the cost of total loss has to be
  specified, or Pillar 4 is unmet and the game is merely safe.

  Derived from 6.6's inheritance rules, because those rules are what make a loss
  a loss:

    TITLES AND SEATS   Gone. No council, no court, no income, no claim
                       leverage. This is the large part of the cost.
    HOUSE STANDING     Survives. So recovery is genuinely possible, and a
                       dynasty that fell can be rebuilt - slowly, and from the
                       bottom of the ladder rather than the middle.
    RELATIONSHIPS      Do not survive. The fallen character begins with an
                       empty social graph, and must rebuild one from nothing.
    TIME               The real currency. Every month of rebuilding is a month
                       not spent on the position that was lost.

  That last bullet is the whole mechanism. Loss hurts because rebuilding is
  slow, not because a counter went down. It also means the pain scales with how
  much the player had - a ruler who falls from a great position loses more
  months of recovery than a knight who falls from a small one - which is the
  correct shape for a consequence.

  And note what is NOT on that list: traits, skills and relationships earned
  personally survive the fall, because they were never the ruler's property to
  begin with. The character who lost a duchy is still the person who survived a
  siege.

WHAT THIS SETTLES ELSEWHERE
  It removes the strongest argument for a traditional defeat condition in 6.17.
  A war that can be lost with no possibility of run-ending consequence has to be
  expensive in a different currency - supply, manpower, months, standing - and
  6.17's campaign layer is where that cost has to live.

OPEN QUESTIONS
  - If a house is extinguished entirely - every title lost, no claimant, no
    standing worth the name - does the run end, or does the player continue as
    a character with no house at all? The latter is consistent with the above
    and is the default assumption until decided.
  - Under PERMANENT, does the run's end report anything? A campaign summary of
    what the dynasty achieved across generations is a distinct question from
    whether the run continues.

6.23 LAND AND TERRAIN                                   [PARTIAL]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  LAND is one of the six pillars of the design model in section 3, and until now
  it had no entry of its own. Terrain is not scenery and not a movement tax. It
  is the thing that decides what a piece of ground is FOR, and therefore who
  wants it and what it is worth.

TERRAIN INFLUENCES WHATEVER IT MAKES SENSE TO INFLUENCE
  The governing principle, and deliberately a mechanism rather than a rule:
  terrain should feed into any system where the ground plausibly matters, and
  those connections should be added as they are found rather than nominated in
  advance. A fixed list of terrain effects designed now would be wrong in the
  same way a fixed list of generated cultures would be - it would not know what
  the game grew into.

  What already follows from it:

    MOVEMENT       Cost and time, and whether movement is possible at all.
                   6.13 already prefers roads and falls back to pathfinding;
                   terrain is what it is pathfinding around.
    YIELD          What a holding can produce, and therefore what it is worth
                   and what it can feed.
    BUILDING       What improvements are possible, and what they cost. Held
                   places already have levelled improvements in 6.8.
    DEFENCE        Where a host can be attacked, and how easily.
    SUPPLY         What an army can eat where, and whether it can reach it.

  The world is generated with varied terrain for exactly this reason: variation
  is what makes these influences visible and worth deciding about.

SUPPLIES FOLLOW THE MAP
  Goods and armies move along the map. Production in one place can only be
  consumed where the roads and rivers reach, and an army in the field eats from
  whatever territory it is standing in or adjacent to.

  This is what makes 6.17's campaign layer real. Attrition is not an abstract
  monthly subtraction - it is the consequence of where an army actually is and
  whether the land around it can feed it. A commander who besieges well can win
  without a battle, and one who marches into empty country can lose without
  losing a single engagement. Cutting a road is therefore a real strategic act
  with a real cost to whoever depended on it.

  It also connects this section to 6.11's economic problem. There are currently
  no expenses and no scarcity, which means nothing in the game has to move
  anywhere. Supply is the mechanism that makes movement matter to a character who
  owns nothing and is not at war - which is most characters.

OPEN QUESTIONS  [OPEN]
  - How does terrain actually change? Deliberately undecided. A river silting, a
    forest cleared for farms, a marsh drained and a war-devastated region are all
    plausible, and any of them needs a cost, a timescale and a legibility story
    so the player can tell permanent landscape from temporary damage.
  - What exactly does fertility yield, and to whom? If terrain sets yield, then
    holding land well is a real long project, and that project needs designing.
  - How are terrain types generated, and does 6.20's terrain-affinity generation
    decide the distribution or merely colour it in?

WHAT IS MISSING
  - Terrain exists and blocks movement, but nothing consumes it. No yield, no
    supply, no building constraints from ground type.

6.24 HEALTH, INJURY AND MORTALITY                        [ABSENT]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  Health is what makes a life finite, and a finite life is what makes succession
  matter. It also connects 6.2's open question about ageing, 6.5's child
  mortality, and 6.17's casualties into one system rather than three accidents.

  Longevity must remain uncertain. Natural mortality is a baseline. Illness,
  wounds, and chance events can strike anyone. Care (physicians, faith-based
  institutions, environment, resources) can improve survival odds, but never
  guarantees it. This prevents keeping favorites alive from becoming trivial.

THREE KINDS OF CONSEQUENCE, AND THE SETTING CHOOSES THE MIX
  A wound in this game does not resolve in one way. It is weighted heavily
  toward healing, but the severe cases are permanent, and the very severe cases
  end a career - and which of those a player lives in is a property of the
  generated setting rather than a constant.

    HEALING          The common case. An injury costs days away from duties,
                     perhaps a season, and then the character is what they were.
                     This is the default weight, because most wounds in any
                     plausible world are not serious and treating them as
                     permanent would be both false and tedious.

    PERMANENT        Serious wounds leave a lasting mark - a damaged joint caps
                     Combat Strength for life, an illness lingers, a siege costs
                     years. The character continues, altered, and the alteration
                     is visible for the rest of the campaign.

    CAREER-ENDING    The worst cases remove a capability entirely. A character
                     who cannot hold a sword cannot lead a company again, no
                     matter how much renown they have.

LETHALITY IS A SETTING PROPERTY
  This is the part that ties the three together: the rate at which wounds kill,
  and the rate at which they leave permanent affliction, is a property of the
  world rather than of the code. Medicine, sanitation, belief and era all bear on
  it, which makes it a natural companion to the Start Era and Technology
  parameters in 6.20. Care improves odds but never guarantees survival.

  A generated world with poor medicine produces short lives, brutal dynasties and
  a great deal of succession crisis. One with good medicine produces long lives and
  a different kind of tedium - heirs who never die of fever and a dynasty that
  outlasts the player's interest in it. Both are legitimate settings, and the
  player should be able to feel which one they generated without being told.

MORTALITY IS NOT ONLY VIOLENCE
  Ageing, illness and childbirth kill more people than battles do, and the system
  should say so. This is the open question in 6.2 about whether there is a hard
  cap by age and whether physical skills decay; those questions belong here as
  much as to 6.2, and the answer should be that age is a slow decline that
  interacts with wounds rather than a cliff.

  Childbirth and childhood illness belong here too, and they are the mechanism
  behind 6.5's decision that children die. They should be lethal enough that a
  large family is genuine insurance, because that is what makes heir production a
  strategy rather than a formality.

WHAT IS MISSING
  - No health, no injury, no mortality beyond whatever ageing tick already does.
  - No casualties in 6.17, and therefore no reason for a character to be marked
    by a war.

6.25 THE PLAYER'S VIEW                                   [DESIGN]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INTENT
  What the player looks at is a design obligation under Pillar 5, not a UI
  preference. A player who cannot see why something happened cannot understand
  the game, whatever the game is doing correctly underneath.

A CONSTANT BIRDSEYE MAP
  The map is always present and shows the whole world. The player zooms in for
  detail and out for strategic overview, and their own character and concerns are
  tracked with indicators on it while the surrounding world is managed through
  interface panels rather than by scrolling.

  This is the right shape for a game whose subject is a person inside a dynasty,
  because the tension between "where am I" and "what am I responsible for" is the
  tension of the whole game. A character is one figure on a large map they do not
  control.

WHAT THE MAP HIDES IS A PLAYER SETTING
  The standing question is how much of the world is visible, and the honest
  answer is that it is a setting, because the three modes support genuinely
  different games and there is no single correct one:

    FULL REVEAL            Everything visible everywhere, including who rules
                           what and how they feel. Best for players who want a
                           strategy game and want to plan in the open.

    REVEAL, LOCAL ACTION   The whole map is visible, but the player may only
                           act directly on their own sphere of influence.
                           Visible does not mean actionable.

    REVEAL BY CONTACT      The world starts unknown and is learned through
                           exploration, rumour and contact. This is the mode
                           that makes 6.8's discovery states load-bearing for the
                           entire map rather than for caves alone.

  Making this a setting rather than a decision also resolves a tension that
  otherwise sits badly in the document. Section 5 asks whether the player should
  be able to see the simulation's edges, and answers that it would be more honest.
  A full birdseye map implies the player can see the whole world at once, which
  is more than the relevance-based simulation gives them. Rather than forcing one
  answer, the setting lets the player choose how much they are shown - and the
  game never lies to them about what it is withholding, which is the part that
  actually matters for legibility.

  What must be honest under all three is the difference between "there is
  something in that ruin" and "there is nothing in that ruin". Uncertainty the
  player is told about is fair; a map that quietly lies about the world is not.

WHAT THE PLAYER SEES ABOUT PEOPLE
  Characters are shown at the fidelity their relevance earns, per section 5. A
  relative or a spouse is a full sheet. A distant ruler is a name, a title and a
  reported disposition. The player's own indicator should always be legible, and
  a change in someone's opinion of the player should always be traceable to
  something that happened - never to unexplained drift.



WHAT IS MISSING
  - No player-facing view of any kind is specified in this document, and none of
    the above exists.
